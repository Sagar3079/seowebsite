The Chef

Elias Marsh cooks by ear

After twelve years on gas ranges in Manhattan — most recently as chef de cuisine at a two-star kitchen he still won't name — Elias shut off the valve. He spent a year staging at asadors in the Basque country, learning to read a fire the way most chefs read a ticket rail: by sound, by smell, by the color at the edge of the coals.

At Ember & Oak he cooks forty covers a seating with a brigade of five, a four-ton brick hearth, and a woodpile he stacks himself every Tuesday. No thermometers on the pass. If you ask him how he knows the duck is ready, he'll tell you the fat changes its pitch.

"Gas is convenient. Oak is honest."

The Hearth

What the fire asks of us

14

months seasoning

Every split of white oak is stacked and air-dried in our barn across the river before it earns a place in the hearth.

620°

at the grate

The hearth is lit at 1 pm and raked into three zones — searing, roasting, and a bed of embers for the slow work.

9

farms within 30 miles

Duck from Waylan Farm, greens from Sparrowbush, flour milled at Wild Hive. The fire deserves good company.
